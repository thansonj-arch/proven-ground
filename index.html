Food Order & Foods to Avoid

The Science of Meal Sequencing

Shukla et al. (2015, Diabetes Care) RCT: eating vegetables and protein BEFORE carbohydrates reduced postprandial glucose by 29% at 30 min and 37% at 60 min, and reduced insulin response by 37%. GLP-1 (satiety hormone) was significantly higher. Replicated in prediabetes (Shukla et al., 2019).

Practical Order — Every Meal

① Non-starchy vegetables (salad, broccoli, spinach) → ② Protein (chicken, fish, eggs) → ③ Fat (olive oil, avocado, nuts) → ④ Starchy carbs LAST (rice, potato, bread)

Examples

Breakfast: Greek yogurt + berries → then oatmeal
Lunch: Side salad → grilled chicken → then rice
Dinner: Roasted vegetables → salmon → then sweet potato

Foods to Limit
1. Sugar-sweetened beverages — 26% increased T2DM risk per daily serving (Malik et al., 2010)
2. White bread & refined carbs — rapid glucose spikes, worsens insulin resistance
3. Processed meats — 42% increased T2DM risk (Pan et al., 2011)
4. Fried foods / trans fats — raise LDL, lower HDL
5. Fruit juice — glycemic response similar to soda (no fiber buffer)
6. Packaged snacks — refined carbs + seed oils + hidden sodium
7. Excess alcohol — raises triglycerides, impairs hepatic glucose output
8. High-sodium condiments — aim <2300mg/day (DASH trial)
Intermittent Fasting & Time-Restricted Eating (TRE)

What Is TRE?

Time-restricted eating limits food intake to a consistent daily window — typically 8-10 hours — aligned with circadian biology. This is distinct from caloric restriction; the benefit comes from the timing itself. Satchin Panda's research at the Salk Institute has been central to this field.

The Evidence

Sutton et al. (2018, Cell Metabolism): early TRE (6-hour window, eating done by 3pm) improved insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, and oxidative stress in prediabetes — without weight loss. Wilkinson et al. (2020): 10-hour TRE over 12 weeks reduced weight, blood pressure, and LDL in metabolic syndrome. Meta-analysis (Liu et al., 2022, Obesity Reviews): TRE consistently reduced fasting glucose and body weight.

Circadian Alignment Matters

Earlier eating windows (e.g. 7am–5pm or 8am–6pm) outperform late windows (12pm–8pm) for metabolic outcomes, because insulin sensitivity follows a circadian rhythm and peaks in the morning. Eating late disrupts circadian gene expression.

Practical Approaches

16:8 — Fast 16 hours, eat within an 8-hour window (most popular, good starting point)
14:10 — More sustainable, still significant benefit. Good entry point.
Early TRE — 7am–3pm or 8am–4pm, strongest metabolic data but hardest to maintain socially.
5:2 — 5 normal days, 2 days of very low calorie (500–600 kcal). Equivalent outcomes to daily TRE in some trials.

Who Should Be Cautious

Those with a history of eating disorders, pregnant or breastfeeding women, those on insulin or sulfonylureas (hypoglycemia risk), and underweight individuals should consult a physician before starting TRE.

Key practical tip: Start with a 12-hour fast (e.g. 7pm–7am) — this alone improves overnight glucose clearance and aligns with natural circadian biology. Build from there.
Post-Meal Walking

The Evidence

DiPietro et al. (2013, Diabetes Care): three 15-minute post-meal walks reduced 24-hour blood glucose significantly more than a single 45-minute morning walk. The effect was most pronounced after the evening meal. Mechanism: skeletal muscle contraction increases GLUT4 translocation to the cell membrane independent of insulin — a non-insulin-mediated glucose disposal pathway.

How Much?

Even 10 minutes at a casual pace produces measurable glucose-lowering effects. The benefit is dose-dependent — 15-20 minutes is optimal. Intensity matters less than getting moving; a gentle walk works.

Practical Strategy

Build a post-meal walk into your routine as a non-negotiable habit. After dinner especially — this is when the largest glucose spike of the day typically occurs and when insulin sensitivity is lowest (circadian nadir). A 15-minute after-dinner walk can reduce that spike by 30-50% compared to sitting.

Beyond Glucose

Post-meal walking also improves digestion and gut motility, reduces triglyceride response to a meal, supports circadian alignment, and accumulates meaningful daily step counts without dedicated exercise time.

Simple target: 10–15 minutes of walking within 30 minutes of each main meal. Park farther away, take stairs, or loop around the block — it all counts.

Why Sleep Is the Foundation

Metabolic Health

Spiegel et al. (1999, Lancet): just 6 days of sleep restriction to 4 hours impaired glucose tolerance by 40% and elevated cortisol — equivalent to a decade of aging in metabolic terms. Sleep deprivation reduces insulin sensitivity via elevated cortisol and growth hormone dysregulation. For prediabetes management, sleep is not optional.

Body Composition

Nedeltcheva et al. (2010, Annals of Internal Medicine): subjects sleeping 5.5 hours vs 8.5 hours lost the same weight on a diet, but 55% of weight loss in the short-sleep group was lean mass vs. 25% in the full-sleep group. Poor sleep preserves fat and destroys muscle — the opposite of recomposition.

Hormonal Cascades

Sleep deprivation increases ghrelin (hunger hormone) +24%, decreases leptin (satiety hormone) -18% (Taheri et al., 2004, PLOS Medicine), and elevates cortisol — all driving increased caloric intake, fat storage, and muscle breakdown. Matthew Walker describes this as "the perfect storm for weight gain."

Cardiovascular & Longevity

Cappuccio et al. (2011) meta-analysis: sleeping <6 hours associated with 48% increased risk of cardiovascular disease and 15% increased all-cause mortality. The relationship is U-shaped — both under and over sleeping (>9 hrs) carry risk.

Target: 7–9 hours of sleep is the evidence-based sweet spot for most adults (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015 — National Sleep Foundation consensus).
Sleep Architecture

Light Sleep (N1 & N2) — ~50% of sleep

Transition to deeper sleep. N2 features sleep spindles important for memory consolidation. The body begins temperature drop and heart rate slows. Not restorative on its own but necessary as a gateway to deeper stages.

Deep Sleep / Slow Wave Sleep (N3) — ~20-25%

The most physically restorative stage. Growth hormone secretion peaks here — critical for muscle repair, fat metabolism, and immune function. Primarily in the first half of the night. Alcohol, even moderate amounts, suppresses SWS by up to 24% (Ebrahim et al., 2013). This is where physical recovery happens.

REM Sleep — ~20-25%

Brain consolidates memory, processes emotions, and performs synaptic maintenance. REM is when dreaming occurs and emotional regulation is restored. REM pressure builds across the night — cutting sleep short eliminates disproportionate amounts of REM. Matthew Walker: "REM sleep is emotional first aid."

Sleep Cycles

Each cycle is ~90 minutes. A full night has 4-6 cycles. Early cycles are dominated by deep sleep; later cycles by REM. Waking after 6 hours misses the REM-rich final 2 hours disproportionately. Try to complete full cycles — wake at 90-minute intervals for less grogginess.

Evidence-Based Sleep Optimization

1. Temperature — The Most Powerful Lever

Core body temperature must drop 1-2°F to initiate and maintain sleep. Walker: "You cannot fall asleep if your core temp doesn't drop." Keep bedroom at 65-67°F (18-19°C). A warm bath or shower 1-2 hours before bed paradoxically speeds sleep onset by pulling blood to extremities, accelerating core cooling.

2. Light — Circadian Master Signal

Morning sunlight (within 30-60 minutes of waking) anchors your circadian clock via retinal ganglion cells and melanopsin. This determines your cortisol peak and evening melatonin onset. Evening blue light from screens blocks melatonin by up to 50% and delays its onset by 3 hours (Gooley et al., 2011). Use blue light blocking glasses after sunset, or set devices to "Night Mode."

3. Consistency — Regularity Beats Duration

Your wake time is the primary anchor for your circadian rhythm. Even on weekends, a consistent wake time (within 30 min) prevents "social jet lag" — which impairs insulin sensitivity, mood, and cognitive function Monday through Thursday. Set a non-negotiable wake time.

4. Caffeine — Later Than You Think

Caffeine's half-life is 5-7 hours. A 2pm coffee still has 50% activity at 9pm. Adenosine (sleep pressure molecule) builds during the day — caffeine masks it but doesn't clear it. The "sleep debt" remains. Cut caffeine by 1pm for most people, 12pm if sensitive.

5. Alcohol — Sleep Quality Destroyer

Alcohol is sedating but fragments sleep, suppresses REM, and reduces SWS by 24% (Ebrahim et al., 2013). It dramatically increases snoring and sleep apnea severity. Even 1-2 drinks meaningfully impairs recovery sleep. Avoid within 3 hours of bed.

6. Exercise Timing

Regular exercise is one of the most effective interventions for sleep quality. However, vigorous exercise within 2 hours of bed can delay sleep onset via elevated core temperature and cortisol. Zone 2 cardio is ideal in the morning — it improves adenosine sensitivity and deepens slow-wave sleep.

7. Key Supplements for Sleep

Magnesium glycinate (200-400mg at bedtime): reduces cortisol, improves sleep quality and duration (Abbasi et al., 2012).
Ashwagandha (300-600mg KSM-66): reduces cortisol 27%, improves sleep onset (Salve et al., 2019).
Low-dose melatonin (0.5-1mg, not 5-10mg): signals circadian shift, does not induce sleep. Best for jet lag or delayed sleep phase.
L-theanine (200mg): promotes relaxation without sedation, reduces sleep onset latency.

HRV & Recovery

What Is HRV?

Heart Rate Variability measures the variation in time between heartbeats (in milliseconds). Higher HRV = more adaptable autonomic nervous system = better recovery, stress tolerance, and cardiovascular fitness. It's captured by Apple Watch, Oura Ring, Garmin, and Whoop.

HRV as a Recovery Signal

HRV reflects the balance between sympathetic ("fight or flight") and parasympathetic ("rest and digest") activity. A low HRV morning reading indicates your body is still recovering from yesterday's stressor (training, stress, alcohol, illness). Flinn et al. (2014): HRV-guided training produced greater fitness gains than predetermined programs, because athletes rested when recovery was incomplete.

Normal Ranges

HRV is highly individual — your trend matters more than absolute numbers. Men aged 40-50 typically range 30-60ms. More important: if your HRV drops >10-15% below your personal baseline, treat it as a recovery day (Zone 2 or rest). If elevated, it's a green light for intensity.

What Improves HRV

Regular Zone 2 aerobic training (strongest evidence), consistent sleep schedule, HRV-biofeedback breathing (5-6 breaths/min, "coherence breathing"), cold exposure, magnesium supplementation, stress reduction, reduced alcohol, and maintaining healthy body weight all improve baseline HRV.

What Suppresses HRV

Alcohol (even 1-2 drinks), sleep deprivation, overtraining, high psychological stress, illness, inflammatory foods, dehydration, and extreme caloric deficits all reduce HRV. Monitoring trends reveals which lifestyle factors most affect your personal recovery.
